# The Bloody Banana Empire Is Starting to Crumble

Around 80% of the world's exported bananas come from just five countries. That concentration is not an accident. It has a dark history.

In 1910, a banana seller named Samuel Zemurray bought a former U.S. Navy ship, filled it with mercenaries, and helped overthrow the government of Honduras to protect his business. The invasion worked. It became one of the most extraordinary examples of how American fruit companies came to dominate entire countries, and Honduras became a textbook example of what people later called a "banana republic."

But today, that empire is starting to crumble. A microscopic fungus is spreading through the world's plantations, and it can't be bribed or overthrown.

## How bananas took over the world

Bananas originally came from tropical Asia, where people have been growing them for thousands of years. For most of history, they were eaten close to where they grew. They bruise easily and ripen fast, which makes them nearly impossible to ship across oceans.

That began to change in the late 1800s. In the 1870s, an American named Minor Keith was building a railroad through Costa Rica, a project so brutal that thousands of workers died building it. He planted bananas along the tracks to feed his crews, then realized he could ship them north. Americans were just discovering the fruit, and demand was high. In 1899, Keith merged with the Boston Fruit Company to form the United Fruit Company.

Over the following decades, United Fruit owned the land, planted the bananas, and owned the transportation to get them everywhere: the railroads, the ships, everything. In some countries it controlled so much that local people began calling it *El Pulpo*, "The Octopus."

Governments cut deals with the companies, offering cheap land and tax breaks to keep them doing business in their country. And as the industry expanded, one type of banana came to dominate: Gros Michel, or "Big Mike." It produced large bunches of sweet fruit with thick skins that were especially well suited to shipping.

But there was a major problem with how these bananas were grown. Bananas don't have seeds, so plantations are planted from clones of existing plants. That means every plant in a field comes from the same parent. If a disease can infect one, it can potentially infect them all.

By 1890, that started to happen. A soil fungus known as Panama disease began spreading through plantations in Central America. It entered through the roots. The leaves turned yellow, wilted, and collapsed, killing the plant. Worst of all, the fungus stayed in the ground for decades.

When the disease arrived, companies often ditched the land and started over, planting the same Gros Michel bananas on new ground. Eventually the fungus reached those plantations too, and the cycle repeated. It was an unsustainable way to cultivate land, but the land was cheap.

Between 1890 and the mid-1950s, roughly 100,000 acres of Gros Michel plantations were destroyed or abandoned across Central and South America. Entire communities that depended on those plantations could lose their livelihoods when the companies moved on. Enormous stretches of tropical rainforest were cleared to make room for new plantations, railroads, and ports. And because the companies knew a plantation might eventually become unusable, they began buying far more land than they actually needed.

Getting that much land required cooperation from local governments. And because they controlled the jobs, the railroads, the ships, and the ports, banana companies had enormous power over the countries where they operated. They were becoming powerful enough to shape the political and economic futures of entire nations.

## The man who overthrew Honduras

By 1910, Honduras was one of the biggest banana exporters in Central America. Samuel Zemurray, a Russian-born immigrant, had made his first fortune selling bananas in the United States. He expanded, founding the Cuyamel Fruit Company, and cut deals with the Honduran government that gave him tax breaks on machinery he imported into the country.

Then came a threat to those deals. Honduras owed a lot of money to Europe, and the United States was planning to bail it out with a $10 million loan backed by J.P. Morgan. Under the terms of the agreement, the U.S. would control Honduras's customs revenue and the taxes collected at its ports. Zemurray did not want Washington in control. He wanted to keep his special treatment.

So he teamed up with the former president, Manuel Bonilla, who was living in exile in New Orleans, to overthrow the government. He bought an old Navy ship, the *Hornet*, and filled it with mercenaries led by Lee Christmas and Guy "Machine Gun" Molony.

On January 10, 1911, the rebels captured the Honduran port of Trujillo — the same day the U.S. signed the loan agreement with Honduras. Over the next two weeks they fought their way along the coast and captured the major banana port of La Ceiba. The plan worked. Under pressure from the rebels, the Honduran Congress voted 32 to 4 to reject the loan, and Zemurray got Bonilla back into power. In return, Zemurray received tens of thousands of acres of land and kept his tax breaks.

It wasn't just governments. In 1928, 25,000 United Fruit workers in Colombia went on strike, demanding real wages and fair treatment. The army was sent in, and on December 6, soldiers opened fire on the strikers. To this day, no one knows exactly how many were killed. Estimates range from dozens to hundreds or more.

A few decades later, in Guatemala, a similar story played out. In 1952, a democratic leader, Jacobo Árbenz, was pushing for the large fruit companies to sell unused land back to the government so local people could manage it themselves. Estimates at the time found that around 85% of United Fruit's land in Guatemala was unused. Guatemala offered to pay for the land based on the value United Fruit had declared for taxes, roughly $1 million. The company demanded much more.

United Fruit leaned on the U.S. government, and in 1954 the CIA organized a coup that overthrew Árbenz. Talks of land reform ended. Zemurray had built his empire by helping to overthrow a government to get his way. Now his company had helped bring down another.

But all that power could not solve the core problem on the plantations. The same fungus that had pushed the companies to grab more and more land was still destroying their trees.

## The disease that could change bananas again

Growers switched to the Cavendish because Gros Michel was no longer working. They thought Panama disease had been left in the past. But Cavendish bananas are clones too.

Then a new disease arrived: a new strain of the same fungus. History repeated itself a few decades later. Scientists call it Fusarium wilt Tropical Race 4, or TR4. It travels through contaminated soil and water, including mud on work boots, tools, and farm machinery. Once land is infected, the fungus can again stay there for decades. There is still no practical way to get rid of TR4 in a contaminated field.

For many years the disease was concentrated in parts of Asia. More recently it reached Latin America, where most of the world's exported bananas are grown. Between 2019 and 2023, it was confirmed in Colombia, Peru, and Venezuela.

The biggest concern came in December 2025. Ecuador, the world's largest banana exporter, officially confirmed its first outbreak of TR4. Authorities quarantined the affected farm, hoping to contain the fungus. In July 2026, a second infected plantation was discovered nearby. Flooding in the outbreak zone has already overtopped containment works, a reminder of how easily soil and water can carry the fungus to new fields.

Scientists are working on solutions. In Australia, researchers have developed a genetically modified banana, though it is not being grown commercially yet. In Honduras, scientists are developing disease-resistant varieties through traditional breeding. But finding a banana that resists TR4 is only half the challenge. It also has to survive shipping, ripen consistently, taste good, and be cheap enough to grow on a massive scale. Replacing the Cavendish across the global industry would be a huge job.

So can the banana survive the industry that built it?

## You reap what you sow

For more than a century, banana companies built enormous fortunes by exploiting workers, influencing governments, and clearing vast areas of tropical rainforest. When disease destroyed their plantations, they could abandon the land and move somewhere else. For decades, that strategy worked.

But the industry's obsession with efficiency and short-term profits created a system that was never built to last. First, Panama disease devastated the Gros Michel. Now a new strain threatens the Cavendish.

Scientists are developing resistant varieties, and bananas aren't about to disappear from the shelves. But the industry may have to change, fundamentally, how it grows them. There is only so much land to clear, and only so long you can keep repeating the same mistakes.

Eventually, you reap what you sow.
