# Why Great White Sharks Fail in Captivity

In 2004, a young great white shark arrived at the Monterey Bay Aquarium. She had been caught off the coast of Southern California, still small enough to transport and young enough to survive conditions that had defeated almost every earlier attempt.

Captivity had not been kind to her species. Some great whites refused to eat. Some injured themselves against tank walls. Others never seemed to adjust to an enclosed space at all. Before Monterey, no great white shark had survived in an aquarium for more than 16 days.

This one would last 198. And what happened next explains why you still can't see a great white shark in any aquarium today.

## A shark that couldn't go in a normal tank

Adult great white sharks can grow to around 20 feet long. Monterey knew it was working against decades of failure. By the aquarium's own count, there had been dozens of unsuccessful attempts at public aquariums going back to the 1950s. In most of them, the shark stopped feeding and either died or had to be released within days.

So this was not an animal that could be dropped into a normal tank and treated like another exhibit. The aquarium went after a juvenile, a "young of the year," because younger sharks had shown they acclimate more readily than adults. She was held for weeks in a huge ocean pen off Southern California before she ever saw a wall.

Then she went into the aquarium's Outer Bay exhibit, now called the Open Sea: a tank holding more than a million gallons of water, built for animals that spend their lives moving through open water. Tuna, sea turtles, and other large swimmers already shared the space. For a great white shark, it was one of the few captive environments that even had a chance of working.

## 198 days behind the glass

For the first few months, it worked. The shark swam, fed, and grew. The morning after she arrived, she ate, the first time a white shark had ever taken food on exhibit.

Visitors saw something almost nobody had seen before: a living great white shark moving through an aquarium. Not a preserved jaw, not a distant fin, not an animal glimpsed for a moment in open water, but a real body passing through the blue behind the glass. Nearly a million people came to see her.

She stayed for 198 days. That is still the longest a great white shark has ever been successfully exhibited, and it's recognized by Guinness World Records. Monterey hadn't solved captivity, but it had reached a point no aquarium had reached before.

But the longer she stayed, the more obvious the limits became.

## The animal you bring in isn't the one you keep

A great white shark does not stay the same animal you bring into the tank. It grows quickly. Its strength changes, its diet changes, and its behavior changes.

The young shark in Monterey's exhibit wasn't just getting bigger. In 198 days she went from about 5 feet and 62 pounds to more than 6 feet and 162 pounds. She was becoming more like the predator her body was built to be.

Eventually, her behavior toward other animals became a concern. In early 2005 she bit and killed two soupfin sharks that shared her exhibit, and staff began to see her actively hunting. On March 31, 2005, the aquarium fitted her with an electronic tag and released her back into the ocean.

That release is what separates Monterey's attempt from a simple captivity story. The aquarium had done better than anyone, but the result did not prove that great white sharks belong behind glass. It showed how narrow the window was. Under unusually careful conditions, in one of the largest exhibits available, and with a young shark that could still physically fit the space, captivity worked for a limited time. Then the animal began moving toward a life the tank could not provide.

## The problem isn't size, it's range

The problem is not just that great white sharks are big. Aquariums keep large animals. It's not just that they're dangerous, either. Aquariums keep dangerous animals, too.

The problem is that a great white shark is built around range. Its life depends on long movements, changing water, hunting, migration, and a constant stream of information from the ocean around it.

A tank can hold seawater, control temperature, manage oxygen, and provide food. It can be built with smooth curves instead of sharp corners. It can reduce some risks and imitate parts of the ocean's chemistry. But it can't become the Pacific.

It can't give an animal thousands of miles to move through. It can't recreate the long routes between feeding grounds, the changing pressure of depth, the smell of prey carried across open water, or the option to leave when something feels wrong.

Great white sharks read the ocean in ways humans barely notice. They respond to vibration, smell, pressure, movement, and faint electrical signals given off by other animals. In the wild, those signals lead somewhere. A shark can follow, turn away, dive, accelerate, disappear, or keep traveling.

In a tank, those choices collapse into a loop.

## When an adult was tried

That's why so many attempts have failed. In January 2016, the Okinawa Churaumi Aquarium in Japan put a roughly 11.5-foot (3.5-meter) great white shark on display after it was accidentally caught in a fishing net. It swam normally for a couple of days, then suddenly weakened and sank to the bottom of the tank. It died after three days.

*In 2016, a great white shark put on display at Okinawa Churaumi Aquarium died after just three days.*

That case was more extreme than Monterey's, but it showed the same basic problem from the other direction. An adult great white shark is not just harder to keep. It may be the wrong kind of animal to keep at all.

## Its habitat is distance

Some aquarium animals adapt because their natural lives happen on a smaller scale. A reef fish may spend much of its life around one section of coral. Some sharks stay close to reefs, the seafloor, or predictable territories. Their world can be shrunk without completely changing what they are.

A great white shark's world is different. Its habitat isn't just water. Its habitat is distance.

That is what Monterey's experiment revealed. The aquarium came closer than anyone by using a juvenile shark, giving her enormous space, monitoring her carefully, and releasing her when the exhibit stopped being appropriate. Monterey went on to display several more young white sharks through 2011, each for a temporary stay and each eventually released. Then it ended the program, and it now says it will not display white sharks in the future.

The most successful attempt didn't lead to a permanent display. It led to a boundary.

## Why no aquarium has one today

That's why no aquarium is known to keep a great white shark today. It isn't for lack of interest. The animal is famous enough that an aquarium with a healthy adult great white would draw enormous crowds.

But Monterey's best attempt showed that keeping one alive is not the same as giving it the conditions its life is built around. Some animals can be brought into aquariums and still remain mostly themselves. A great white shark loses too much in the translation. The ocean isn't just where it lives. It's how it moves, hunts, senses, grows, and survives.

So the absence of great white sharks in aquariums isn't really a failure of technology. It's one of the few cases where humans got close enough to understand an animal, and then stopped trying to turn it into an exhibit.
